The Last Mile

The hardest part of any delivery is the very last stretch.

You're helping a small delivery courier get across town as fast as possible. The city map between the depot and the customer's street has close to twenty different roads, and every single one has its own drive time depending on traffic, stoplights, and distance.

Some routes look short on the map but are slow in real life. Others loop around but save whole minutes at a light. With this many roads branching off each other, there's no way to just eyeball the fastest path — and guessing wrong costs the courier real time on every single delivery.

You need a way to know, for certain, exactly which sequence of roads gets there quickest — before the van even leaves the depot.

How this works

Next, you'll work with a small map of the courier's city — eight locations connected by roads, each labeled with its drive time in minutes.

Before you start
  • You'll first watch a guided walkthrough. Click Next Step and watch the map find its way, one location at a time — always moving to whichever unvisited location is currently fastest to reach.
  • After that, you'll drive it yourself. Click the location you think should be visited next, based on the running distance table.
  • Pick correctly and the route updates in real time. Pick the wrong location and you'll get a hint pointing you back to the table — no penalty, just try again.
  • Watch the side panel as you go — it keeps a running tally of the shortest known time to reach every location on the map, updated live as new roads are discovered.

What you just did: Dijkstra's algorithm

In plain terms

Dijkstra's algorithm finds the shortest route from one starting point to every other point in a network, by always taking the smallest guaranteed step next. It keeps a running "shortest known time" for every location, and at each turn it locks in whichever unvisited location currently has the smallest number in that table — because once every cheaper path has already been checked, that number can't get any smaller. Locking it in, then rechecking its neighbors for shortcuts, is what lets the whole map's shortest routes fall into place one location at a time, without ever having to try every possible path by hand.

time(v) = min( time(v), time(u) + road(u, v) )

Whenever a shorter route to a location v is found by passing through a just-visited location u, its time is updated. This single check, repeated over and over, is called "relaxing" the road.

Exercises

A few "what would you do" scenarios — no map required, just the idea.

1
A faster shortcut

A new road opens, connecting the depot directly to a location three streets away, cutting that stretch's time in half. Which entries in the distance table would need to be rechecked, and why?

2
Already visited

A location has already been marked visited with a shortest time of 8 minutes. A moment later, a much faster road leading to it is discovered. Should its time be updated? Why or why not?

3
A tie on the map

Two unvisited locations both currently show a shortest known time of 6 minutes. Does it matter which one gets visited first? What would you check to decide?

4
Unreachable ground

One location has no roads connecting it to anywhere the algorithm has explored so far. What does its entry in the distance table look like, and when — if ever — does that change?

5
Picking the wrong turn

Someone suggests always driving to whichever neighboring location looks physically closest on the map, instead of checking the distance table. Where would that plan break down?
