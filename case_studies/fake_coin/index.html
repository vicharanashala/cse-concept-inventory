Introduction

Why This Matters

In many real-life situations, people face problems too large to handle all at once — sorting a mountain of paperwork, finding a champion among dozens of teams, or narrowing down one item hidden inside a huge collection. Checking or comparing everything one piece at a time is slow, and it only gets worse as the pile grows. A far more effective approach is to split the problem into two smaller, roughly equal parts, deal with (or rule out) one part, and repeat the same splitting on whatever is left — cutting the effort down dramatically at every step.

A few everyday examples of this:

  • Tournament brackets: In a 64-team knockout tournament, nobody compares every team against every other team. Teams are paired up, half are eliminated each round, and the pool of contenders keeps shrinking until one champion remains.
  • Finding a page in a thick book: Instead of flipping through page by page, you open the book somewhere near the middle, decide whether what you want is earlier or later, and repeat — narrowing it down fast.
  • Sorting a huge stack of files: A librarian organizing thousands of documents splits the stack into two smaller stacks, sorts each one separately (splitting further if needed), then combines the sorted results.

The common thread: a big, unwieldy problem gets solved efficiently by repeatedly cutting it down into a much smaller version of itself. Keep that idea in mind as you work through the case study on the next page.

A treasury, a scale, and one fake coin

Somewhere in a large pile of identical gold coins, exactly one is fake — a little lighter than the rest. You have a balance scale. It doesn't tell you a weight, only which side is heavier, or if both sides match. Your job: find the fake coin using as few weighings as possible.

🪙 20 – 1,024 coins, your choice ⚖️ scale shows: left heavier / right heavier / balanced 🎯 goal: fewest weighings

Here's how the game works: pick how many coins you're starting with, then hit weigh. The coins split into two equal pans (and if you picked an odd number, one coin gets set aside instead of being weighed). Watch which side of the scale sinks — that side is heavier, so it's made entirely of real coins. Your job is to eliminate the correct half. Get it right and you keep going with a smaller pile; get it wrong and the fake coin is gone for good. If the pans ever balance exactly, neither one holds the fake — it has to be the coin you set aside. Keep repeating until only one coin is left.

Think it through

1

2

3

Solving smaller parts: What made each smaller problem easier to solve than the original one?

4

Repeating the process: What happened to the size and difficulty of the problems each time you divided them again?

5

Comparing approaches: If you solved the whole problem at once instead, what would become harder or more time-consuming? Why?

Divide and Conquer

Instead of solving a big problem all at once, break it into smaller versions of the same problem, deal with those, and repeat — until what's left is small enough to solve instantly. That's exactly what you did in the game: a big pile became a tiny one, one weighing at a time.

  • Divide — split the problem into smaller equal parts.
  • Conquer — solve (or eliminate) each part, often the same way again.
  • Combine — join the results back together, if needed.

T(n) = T(n/2) + O(1)  →  O(log₂ n)

Cutting a problem in half each step shrinks it exponentially — that's why 1,024 coins only ever took about 10 weighings, not 1,024.

Apply what you've learned

These are open-ended — think through your reasoning, even if there's no single "right" answer.

1

New problem: You have 64 boxes, and one contains a hidden item. You can repeatedly split the boxes into smaller groups. How could you organize the search to avoid checking every box?

2

Recognize the pattern: A problem can be broken into smaller problems that can each be solved independently. What features would make this a good candidate for Divide and Conquer?

3

Choose an approach: You need to find the maximum value in a small list. Would splitting the list necessarily make the solution better? Explain your choice.

4

Spot the misconception: Someone says, "The more pieces we divide a problem into, the more efficient the algorithm becomes." What is wrong with this reasoning?

5

Change the constraints: Your original problem could be split evenly, but now one part is much larger than the others. How might this affect your approach?

6

Compare efficiency: Two algorithms solve the same problem. One repeatedly handles the entire input, while the other works on smaller portions. What would you examine to determine which will scale better?

7

Unfamiliar application: A company wants to find a faulty component in a large network without testing every component. How could the idea of repeatedly narrowing the problem help, and what would determine whether it is efficient?
