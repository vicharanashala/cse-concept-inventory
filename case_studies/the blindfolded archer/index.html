Chapter 1 · The Riddle

🎯 Finding the Right Direction

In many situations, we need to find a value or make a decision that gives us the best possible result, but the right choice is not known beforehand. We may be adjusting a recipe, tuning a setting, aiming at a target, or trying to improve a prediction.

The challenge is that we cannot always know in advance which choice will give us the best result. We have to make a choice, observe what happens, and determine whether we are moving closer to the result we want.

This raises an important question: How can we systematically improve our choices when we do not know the right answer from the beginning? Solving this problem requires a way to use the information from each attempt to decide which direction to move next and how to keep improving the result.

Let's see what that looks like 👇

Chapter 5 · The Fletcher's Workshop

How the search really worked

Tap a card to open it up and see the idea behind what you just did.

🎯 Replay Your Shots

Press play to watch your own attempts line up, in order, and see exactly where each adjustment came from.

Press play to begin.

What you just did, shoot, measure the miss, adjust, repeat, is exactly how machines learn. This strategy is called Gradient Descent. Here's the mapping:

You (the archer)→ the Optimizer
Bow power→ the Parameter being tuned
Distance you missed by→ the Loss (error)
Warmer / colder feedback→ the Gradient (which direction to adjust)
Nudging the power each shot→ a Gradient Descent update step
The blindfold→ never seeing the loss function directly, only local feedback
🧭The Optimizer+
That's you, the archer. In machine learning, the optimizer is the part of the system that keeps adjusting a value, shot after shot, trying to get closer to the best result.
🎚️The Parameter+
Bow power was the one number you kept changing. In a real model, parameters are the internal settings the system tunes until its predictions get better.
📏The Loss (Error)+
The distance you missed by is exactly what's called loss, or error. It's the single number that tells the system how wrong its current guess still is.
🔥The Gradient+
Warmer and colder feedback was really telling you a direction. The gradient is that same idea, it points toward which way to adjust next to reduce the error.
👣The Learning Rate+
Your Adjust +/− buttons controlled step size. Big steps close the gap fast but risk overshooting, small steps are safer but slower. That trade-off is the learning rate.
🔁Iterating to Convergence+
One shot was never going to be enough. Repeating shoot, measure, adjust, again and again, is how both archers and algorithms converge on the answer.
🙈The Blindfold+
You never saw the target directly, only the size and direction of your miss. Most real optimization works exactly like this: local feedback only, never the full picture.
Chapter 6 · New Targets

Put the idea to the test

New situations to think through using what you just discovered.

1 · New ProblemA rescue team needs to find the nearest safe room in a building. The building has many connected rooms, and moving through each corridor takes one step. How could you organize the search so that nearby rooms are checked before farther rooms?
2 · Recognize the PatternA search starts from one location and explores all locations one step away before moving to locations two steps away. What features of a problem would make this type of search useful?
3 · Choose an ApproachYou need to find a target in a graph where every connection has the same cost. Would you prefer to explore one path deeply or explore locations level by level? Explain your choice.
4 · Spot the MisconceptionSomeone says, "BFS always gives the shortest path because it visits every vertex." What is wrong with this reasoning?
5 · Change the ConstraintsBFS is being used to find the shortest route, but now different connections can have different costs. How might this change your choice of search strategy?
6 · Compare EfficiencyTwo searches explore the same graph. One explores locations level by level, while the other follows one path as far as possible before backtracking. What factors would you examine to decide which search is more suitable for a particular problem?
7 · Unfamiliar ApplicationA communication network has several starting points where an alert is detected at the same time. The company wants to know how quickly the alert could reach every other device. How could a level-by-level search help, and what would determine how efficiently it works?
