Chapter 1 · The Puzzle

🤔 Just Think About It

Imagine you're standing in a huge place, a maze, a city, a cave, or a game world. Somewhere around you is something you're looking for: a treasure, a friend, an exit, or a charging station. You cannot see the entire area, so you have to discover new places as you move.

From where you are, you can only see the places directly connected to your current location. As you explore, each place may lead you to several more places, gradually revealing more of the surroundings.

This raises an important question: How can you search through these places so that a nearby target is found as quickly as possible, without unnecessarily exploring places that are much farther away first?

Solving this problem requires a systematic way to decide which places to explore first and which places to leave for later.

Let's see exactly what that looks like 👇

🌳 Watch BFS in Action

Here's the exact same strategy, on a simple tree this time. Press play and watch the order it lights up in.

Press play to begin.

This is Breadth-First Search (BFS)! Here's what you just discovered in the house:

You (the searcher)→ the algorithm doing the traversal
A Room→ a node in the graph
A Doorway / Staircase→ an edge connecting two nodes
"Only nearby rooms glow"→ only unvisited neighbours join the frontier
Checking all rooms on a floor first→ visiting every node at the current level before the next

This level-by-level strategy is exactly why BFS always finds the shortest path in an unweighted graph: nothing farther away is ever checked before everything closer has been. 🎓

🧭The Strategy+
Check every room nearby before going farther. You never skip ahead to a room two floors up while ground-floor rooms are still unchecked. This is what "breadth" means in Breadth-First Search: spreading outward evenly, one full ring at a time.
🔢The Queue+
Those "options from here" chips were a real queue the whole time. New discoveries always joined the back of the line, and the room you were allowed to check next always came from the front. That First-In, First-Out (FIFO) rule alone is what guarantees the level-by-level pattern.
✅Marking Visited+
Every room you checked was locked in as visited, so it never glowed again. Without that, a room could be added to the queue more than once, and in a graph with cycles the search could loop forever.
📏Why It's the Shortest Route+
Because every room on a floor is fully checked before any room on the next floor even joins the queue, the very first time you reach a room is guaranteed to be by the shortest possible path from the Front Door, as long as every step (every doorway) costs the same.
🌍Where You'll See It+
Shortest-route finders, "people you may know" suggestions, web crawlers, network broadcast alerts, and countless puzzle solvers all lean on this exact breadth-first idea: spread outward one full layer at a time.
⚖️Where It Breaks Down+
BFS assumes every connection costs the same one step. The moment some doorways are "farther" than others (different costs), checking rooms in discovery order no longer guarantees the shortest route, a different strategy is needed.

2 · Recognize the PatternA search starts from one location and explores all locations one step away before moving to locations two steps away. What features of a problem would make this type of search useful?

4 · Spot the MisconceptionSomeone says, "BFS always gives the shortest path because it visits every vertex." What is wrong with this reasoning?
5 · Change the ConstraintsBFS is being used to find the shortest route, but now different connections can have different costs. How might this change your choice of search strategy?
6 · Compare EfficiencyTwo searches explore the same graph. One explores locations level by level, while the other follows one path as far as possible before backtracking. What factors would you examine to decide which search is more suitable for a particular problem?
